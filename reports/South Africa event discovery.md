# Fragmented ticketing scatters South Africa's events everywhere

No single place lists South Africa's events because no single company sells them. Quicket, Howler and Webtickets are only one slice. Shoprite-owned Computicket, Ticketpro, Ticketmaster, Resident Advisor, Fixr, Fever, Luma, Meetup and Eventbrite all sell part of the market, along with a growing tail of low-fee newcomers such as TKKT, uTickets, FestFlow and DigiTicket. Each platform's discovery page shows only its own inventory. The most current and curated supply (club nights, run crews, social clubs) often never reaches a ticketing marketplace at all. It lives on Instagram posts and in WhatsApp groups. So locals stitch together weekly editorial roundups (Time Out, which has covered Cape Town, Joburg and Durban weekly since December 2024, plus What's On in Cape Town and In Your Pocket), organiser Instagram accounts and WhatsApp communities. A personal aggregator is technically cheap: three major sources already publish schema.org `Event` JSON-LD, and LLM extraction costs a few dollars a month. A commercial one is a different matter. Event discovery is a well-documented startup graveyard, no SA ticketing platform runs a public affiliate programme, and AllEvents.in already offers a low-quality scraped catalogue of SA cities. The realistic play is a curated, city-first or niche-first weekly digest that links out to ticket pages and is delivered where South Africans already are (WhatsApp, email). A "one-stop shop" is not.

## Retail tills, self-service platforms and global brands split the market three ways

SA ticketing runs on two layers that serve different buyers. The **legacy retail incumbents** sell through supermarket tills to buyers who pay cash. Computicket, a Shoprite subsidiary since 2005, sells in all Shoprite and Checkers stores and selected Usave and House & Home stores ([Shoprite Holdings](https://www.shopriteholdings.co.za/group/brands/computicket.html)). Shoprite calls it the largest ticketing provider in the country. It dominates stadium sport, major tours and theatre. Webtickets sells through Pick n Pay, Boxer and BP Express ([UFS](https://www.ufs.ac.za/humanities/departments-and-divisions/drama-and-theatre-arts-home/productions/how-to-book-for-productions)). Ticketpro, owned by Blue Label Telecoms since 2014, is the official agent for Cricket South Africa and the Lions, Bulls and Sharks rugby franchises ([LinkedIn](https://za.linkedin.com/company/ticketpro-za)). Tracxn lists Ticketpro as "deadpooled", but its live shop pages and 2026 comparisons say otherwise. Computicket also moved downmarket in 2022 with **Computicket Box Office**, a self-service product at 4.5% commission where free events list at no cost ([Memeburn](https://memeburn.com/2022/06/computicket-launches-box-office-self-service-platform/)).

The **self-service online layer** is where most fragmentation happens. The defining deal of the period was Ticketmaster (Live Nation) agreeing to buy **Quicket in July 2024**. Quicket still runs as a standalone Cape Town brand, and in August 2026 Ticketmaster used it to enter Kenya and Uganda ([Pollstar](https://news.pollstar.com/2024/07/24/ticketmaster-expands-business-in-africa-through-quicket-acquisition/); [AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202608260457.html)). Live Nation therefore owns both the arena end (Ticketmaster SA, launched 2022) and the grassroots end (Quicket) of the market. Shoprite's Computicket holds retail and sport, and Howler stays independent in festivals and cashless payments. A long tail of cheaper entrants sits around those three poles. The fee data below comes from TKKT, a 2026 entrant whose comparison is competitor-authored, so treat the numbers as indicative ([TKKT](https://tkkt.co.za/best-ticket-platforms-south-africa)).

| Platform | Niche | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Computicket / Box Office | Sport, tours, theatre; small organisers via Box Office | Shoprite/Checkers tills; largest by its own claim |
| Webtickets | Mass-market, theatre, attractions | Pick n Pay/Boxer tills |
| Ticketpro | Cricket, rugby | Pick n Pay till integration; status disputed by Tracxn |
| Ticketmaster SA | International tours, arenas | Since Apr 2022; owns Quicket |
| Resident Advisor | Club and electronic nights (Cape Town, Joburg, Pretoria) | Co-sold fabric London's Jan 2026 SA shows with Howler |
| Fixr | Curated Cape Town club nights (e.g. The Loft) | Turns up behind organiser Instagram posts |
| Fever | Candlelight concerts, "experiences" in Cape Town | R550–910 tickets |
| Luma, Meetup, Eventbrite | Tech meetups, side events | Luma has a Cape Town city page |
| TKKT, uTickets, FestFlow, DigiTicket, TicketWiz, EventSuite, Tixsa | Low-fee self-service, conferences, festivals | Fees 0–7%; TKKT pays out to the organiser's own Yoco/PayFast/Paystack account |

Each scene maps to its own platforms. Nightlife runs across Howler, RA, Fixr and Quicket, often with the same event co-listed. Theatre runs on Computicket and Webtickets. Sport runs on Computicket, Ticketpro and Ticketmaster. Tech runs on Luma, Meetup and Eventbrite, and is aggregated by dev.events for [Johannesburg](https://dev.events/meetups/AF/ZA/Johannesburg/tech) and [Cape Town](https://dev.events/meetups/AF/ZA/Cape_Town/tech). Workshops and community events run on Quicket. Fever's Candlelight series sits apart at the Norval Foundation and Star Theatre ([Fever](https://feverup.com/m/269468)). The research could not confirm that DICE, Showpass or several other rumoured names operate in SA. Informal sales through Yoco or PayFast payment links and Instagram DMs are widely assumed, but no source documents them. TKKT's bring-your-own-gateway model does hint that this market is real.

## Instagram and weekly roundups carry discovery, not ticket sites

Locals stay in the loop through three overlapping channels: editorial weekly roundups, organiser Instagram accounts, and WhatsApp communities. Ticketing marketplaces mostly act as the checkout behind them.

**Time Out South Africa** is the backbone of the editorial layer. It launched on 1 December 2024 through a franchise deal with Kagiso Media Radio ([Time Out](https://www.timeout.com/about/latest-news/time-out-is-launching-in-south-africa-on-1st-december-112824)). It now publishes weekly "Out and about" weekend lists for [Johannesburg](https://www.timeout.com/johannesburg/news/out-and-about-in-johannesburg-best-things-to-do-this-weekend-101526), [Durban](https://www.timeout.com/durban/news/out-and-about-in-durban-things-to-do-this-weekend-101526) and [Cape Town](https://www.timeout.com/cape-town/news/out-and-about-in-cape-town-things-to-do-this-weekend-031826). It is the only source found that covers all three metros on a consistent weekly cadence. Cape Town is much better served than the other metros. It has What's On in Cape Town (website plus **~118K Instagram followers**) ([whatsonincapetown.com](https://whatsonincapetown.com/things-to-do-this-weekend-in-cape-town/)), Cape Town Magazine's "7 things to do this weekend" ([Cape Town Magazine](https://www.capetownmagazine.com/weekend)), monthly guides from Cape Town Tourism ([capetown.travel](https://www.capetown.travel/whats-on-in-cape-town-in-may-2026/)), a weekend Beehiiv newsletter ([CapeTourism](https://capetourism.beehiiv.com/p/whats-happening-this-weekend-in-cape-town-2382)), a separate whatsoncapetown.co.za listings network ([What's On Cape Town](https://whatsoncapetown.co.za/events/)), and Cape Town Etc, which pushes its weekend roundups through a **WhatsApp Channel** ([Cape Town Etc](https://www.capetownetc.com/lifestyle/things-to-do-weekend-flowers-food-fun/)). Johannesburg has Time Out, In Your Pocket's events calendar and **Thursday-morning newsletter** ([In Your Pocket](https://blog.inyourpocket.com/south-africa/?p=5446)), Gauteng Tourism's gig-guide posts ([gauteng.net](https://www.gauteng.net/whats-on-g/tag/gig-guide/)) and Caxton local-paper weekend lists ([Rosebank Killarney Gazette](https://www.citizen.co.za/rosebank-killarney-gazette/news-headlines/local-news/2026/06/05/top-things-to-do-this-weekend-in-johannesburg/)). **Durban is the thinnest**: Time Out Durban was the only consistently active guide found. Happen (happenlive.co.za) is an active Cape Town and Joburg listings site with a "My plans" feature ([Happen](https://happenlive.co.za/)). Searches turned up no 2026 activity for several familiar names: 2oceansvibe, JHBLive, Joburg.co.za, Durban Live and The Inside Guide. That does not prove they are dead, but none surfaced.

The editorial layer misses the scenes that matter most to a local looking for something specific. **Instagram is the primary channel for curated nightlife and community events.** Time Out's March 2026 guide to Cape Town's curated club scenes lists collectives by their Instagram handles. It notes that Trap House Culture does not "market events traditionally" and tells people to follow its socials, and that tickets for The Loft go through Fixr ([Time Out Cape Town](https://www.timeout.com/cape-town/nightlife/the-best-curated-club-scenes-in-cape-town)). Facebook still matters for psytrance and older party brands such as Hidden Leaf and PHATT. Social clubs follow the same pattern. What's On in Cape Town found that most clubs (Queer Surf CT, Silent Book Club, Craft Club and others) publish on Instagram and take RSVPs through Quicket or Fixr. Socially Gathering shares details "via social media and WhatsApp groups" ([What's On in Cape Town](https://whatsonincapetown.com/social-clubs-in-cape-town/); [whatsoninjoburg.com](https://whatsoninjoburg.com/event/socially-gathering/)). Joburg's run crews (Braamfontein Runners, Thesis Run Cru, Benchwarmers, Rockies) also live on Instagram ([In Your Pocket](https://inyourpocket.com/southafrica/johannesburg/articles/running-in-johannesburg-social-clubs-and-training)). Music fans can also use RA's city guides ([RA](https://ra.co/guide/za/pretoria)) and Songkick's Cape Town metro ([Songkick](https://developer.songkick.com/metro-areas/32788-south-africa-cape-town)).

Delivery habits favour push over browse. WhatsApp reaches roughly **92–94% of SA internet users** ([MyBroadband](https://mybroadband.co.za/news/smartphones/632644-big-whatsapp-changes-in-south-africa.html)). Prepaid data is still costly, at about **R79 for a 30-day 1GB bundle** ([AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202602120144.html)). Local sentiment also points beyond browsing. A widely shared July 2026 r/capetown post called the city "weirdly cliquey" despite endless events, and replies recommended joining recurring clubs (hiking, running, choirs) rather than chasing one-off listings ([Briefly](https://briefly.co.za/people/family-relationships/248388-cape-town-resident-asks-s-hard-real-friends-endless-events/)). An important caveat: no survey data exists on channel shares in SA. The ranking above rests on organiser behaviour and editorial practice, not measured consumer use.

## What you can do today: a one-evening setup

Nobody serves a developer in SA a complete feed. You can get most of the value with a few subscriptions and a small script.

Start with the editorial layer for your city. Subscribe to Time Out's weekly "Out and about" for your metro. Add one local newsletter: In Your Pocket's Thursday mail for Joburg, or the CapeTourism Beehiiv weekend issue and What's On in Cape Town for Cape Town. Follow Cape Town Etc's WhatsApp Channel if you are in the Western Cape. These roundups are useful partly as **seed lists**: Time Out tags organisers' Instagram handles, so two or three weeks of reading produces a list of the 20–50 venues, collectives and clubs that actually match your taste. Follow them, and turn on post notifications for the handful you care about most. For the scenes the editorial layer misses, follow RA's city guide for electronic music, Songkick's Cape Town metro for concerts, and Luma's city page (luma.com/capetown) plus dev.events for tech meetups. Join the WhatsApp communities of the two or three collectives or clubs you actually attend, since organisers keep their most detailed and timely information there. If your real goal is meeting people rather than seeing shows, the local advice is consistent: pick a recurring format such as a run crew or social club over one-off events.

Then automate the structured part. Quicket's `https://www.quicket.co.za/events/` listing is server-rendered and embeds schema.org `Event` JSON-LD with ISO dates, full postal addresses and offers. It paginates (`/events/south-africa/2/`), and its robots.txt has no wildcard disallow on `/events/` (direct probe, Oct 2026, from the research notes). Luma's Cape Town city page embeds an `ItemList` of Events in JSON-LD, and Ticketpro's shop event pages carry Event JSON-LD too. A personal script using httpx plus `extruct` can pull these daily and filter by postal code or locality. It should set a custom User-Agent (Quicket's robots.txt blocks `Python-urllib` by name), cache responses, and stay at a polite rate. It can then emit an `.ics` file you subscribe to, or a Monday-morning email. For Instagram, an Instagram professional account can use Meta's **Business Discovery** API to read public captions and media URLs from other Business or Creator accounts by username ([Meta](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/instagram-api-with-facebook-login/business-discovery)). Pointing it at your followed venues and running poster images through a vision LLM gives you dates, venues and lineups. At Claude Haiku 5.5's $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens ([Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), a personal volume costs cents. Personal, low-volume use of public, factual data carries far less legal exposure than a public product, but the same courtesy rules apply.

## Building the aggregator: data is the easy part, supply and money are not

### Three tiers of sources, from free JSON-LD to Cloudflare walls

No SA platform offers a public, cross-organiser event-search API. **Eventbrite closed its public event search in February 2020** ([Automattic issue #83](https://github.com/Automattic/eventbrite-api/issues/83)). Luma's API only covers calendars you own and needs Luma Plus ([Luma docs](https://docs.luma.com/reference/getting-started-with-your-api)). Quicket has a developer portal that issues API keys, but its endpoints and eligibility could not be read, and it appears to be organiser-oriented ([Quicket Developer Portal](https://developer.quicket.co.za/)). Howler's known API covers cashless top-ups and webhooks, not discovery ([API Evangelist](https://providers.apievangelist.com/providers/howler/)). Ticketmaster's Discovery API exists ([Ticketmaster](https://developer.ticketmaster.com/products-and-docs/apis/getting-started/)), but nobody has verified whether it exposes SA inventory. In practice, sources fall into acquisition tiers. The table below draws on direct HTTP probes recorded in the research notes (19–20 Oct 2026, single-request volume).

| Tier | Sources | Method | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: structured | Quicket listings, Ticketpro event pages, Luma city pages, Meetup sitemaps | Parse JSON-LD | Low, robust |
| B: JS or undocumented JSON | Howler (Vite SPA, no JSON-LD), Computicket (SPA, robots.txt allows all), Eventbrite discovery, RA `/graphql` | Playwright network capture | Medium, brittle |
| C: unstructured | Webtickets (`event.aspx?itemid=`, OpenGraph only), venue sites, Instagram posters | HTML or vision LLM extraction | Medium, noisy |

Resident Advisor is the hard case. Its HTML pages returned **Cloudflare 403s** to plain clients, while an unauthenticated query to its undocumented GraphQL endpoint answered. Getting past Cloudflare with residential proxies or stealth browsers is where legal risk rises sharply (see below), so partner with RA or leave it out. Facebook Events offers no official route: event search was removed from the Graph API in 2018, and reading pages you don't manage requires the gated Page Public Content Access feature ([Graph API changelog](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/graph-api/changelog/version2.11)).

Once the data is in, the pipeline is standard. Use schema.org `Event` as the canonical model, because the best sources already emit it. Keep a canonical event with `sources[]` (platform, URL, price, last-seen), so a show listed on both Quicket and Computicket becomes one event with two ticket offers, which gives users a free price comparison. For deduplication, normalise venues against a curated table of a few hundred SA venues, block candidates on local date ±1 day plus geohash or venue ID, score with fuzzy title and lineup overlap, and send the ambiguous middle band to a cheap LLM judge. Crawl listings daily and re-check events within 14 days every 6–12 hours for sell-outs and cancellations. The research notes estimate the infrastructure at roughly **$15–60 a month plus $3–50 in LLM costs**. Those figures are unsourced and need checking against current vendor pricing. Extraction itself is trivial: 5,000 pages a month on Haiku 5.5 comes to about $3. The real cost is **maintenance time** on the Tier B scrapers, which break silently. Budget for per-source yield monitoring that alerts when any source drops by more than half.

### South African law rewards a "facts plus link-out" design

SA has **no reported court judgment on web scraping**. The main local commentary, from Swart Attorneys, cites no case law ([Mondaq / Swart Attorneys](https://mondaq.com/southafrica/privacy-protection/1430580/data-scraping-from-a-south-african-perspective)). It identifies four risks.

First, the Copyright Act protects **compilations** as literary works, so copying a significant portion of a platform's listings could infringe, and storing images "would likely infringe". Individual facts are not protected, and SA has no EU-style database right.

Second, **POPIA** applies even to publicly available personal information, and it also protects juristic persons ([ungovr](https://law.ungovr.org/ai/za)).

Third, ToS breach and **unlawful competition** support contractual remedies and interdicts, and heavy server load strengthens the complainant's case.

Fourth, there is an untested theory that scraping after a cease-and-desist, or after getting around a block, counts as unlawful "access" under **Cybercrimes Act s2**.

On the other side, **ECT Act s76** shields "information location tools" (linking and indexing services) from damages.

The resulting design is clear. Store only factual fields (title, date, venue, price, ticket URL). Write your own short descriptions. Never copy images, or use organiser-submitted media only. Store no organiser contact details. Publish a privacy notice and a takedown channel, and register an Information Officer. Never create accounts on target platforms, because that turns weak browsewrap terms into enforceable clickwrap. Stop immediately if a site objects. Linking out to the platform's own checkout also blunts the unlawful-competition argument, because you are sending them sales rather than diverting them. US precedent such as *Meta v. Bright Data* (2024), which held that logged-off scraping of public data did not breach Meta's terms ([Quinn Emanuel](https://www.quinnemanuel.com/the-firm/news-events/client-alert-what-does-the-meta-v-bright-data-summary-judgment-ruling-mean-for-web-scraping/)), is encouraging but does not bind SA courts and does nothing about POPIA or copyright. The platforms' own ToS anti-scraping clauses were not read during this research. Read them before you launch anything public.

### The commercial case is weak for a generalist and plausible for a wedge

The international record is blunt. Survivors in event discovery make their money from **ticketing**: Fever, which bought DICE in 2025 and reportedly reached a $5.2B valuation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fever_(app))), RA, Shotgun, Posh, and Eventfinda. Others sell B2B tools to artists, as Bandsintown does. Pure discovery plays mostly died or were sold for their data: Songkick, originally funded by ticket referral fees, went to Suno in November 2025 mainly for user data ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songkick)). Plancast, EventVue, Zvents and Eventful all failed. The structural reasons are well documented. Event inventory is perishable and hyper-local. About 90% of users visit weekly or less. Supply belongs to organisers who don't need you. Every new city is a fresh cold start. Investors have reportedly lost money in the space so often that YC saw an event-discovery startup in nearly every batch, none of them successful ([Hugh Malkin](http://www.hughmalkin.com/blogwriter/2015/9/23/why-no-one-has-solved-event-discovery)). SA makes most of this harder: the market is smaller, supply is fragmented, and much of it sits on Instagram posters. Meanwhile **no SA ticketing platform runs a public affiliate programme**, unlike the UK's Skiddle with its 30-day referral cookie ([Skiddle](https://www.skiddle.com/affiliates/index.php)). Revenue would have to come from negotiated referral deals, promoter-paid featured placement, or eventually your own ticketing.

The local field is also thin, which cuts both ways. Tracxn counts 68 SA events-tech startups, most of them B2B ([Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/explore/events-tech-startups-in-south-africa/__imdTuAwbFjcvv6CcIanzr_5yChKLAA3eVRhADrOYIj8)). The one indie discovery app found, SoWhereTo (iOS, Cape Town/Stellenbosch/Joburg, v1.0 in August 2024), appears unmaintained ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/za/app/sowhereto-event-discovery/id6612007806)). **AllEvents.in already covers SA cities**, and an October 2026 audit of its Cape Town page found duplicated listings, dates that contradict descriptions, a comedy show at 5 am, mixed USD/EUR/ZAR pricing and templated scraped copy ([AllEvents](https://allevents.in/cape%20town/all)). Breadth is therefore already taken, badly. The opening is **accuracy and curation**. Eventfinda is the template that shows this can work in a small market. It launched in New Zealand (about 5M people) in 2006 to unify fragmented listings, then added ticketing, which became the business, and processed 20M tickets in a year by 2017 ([Startup Daily](https://www.startupdaily.net/advice/new-zealand-startup-eventfinda-is-a-ticketing-system-built-on-an-events-discovery-platform/)).

A credible build for one developer looks like this. Pick a wedge: either a city-first guide for **Johannesburg or Durban**, which have thinner editorial coverage than Cape Town, or a niche such as Joburg electronic/amapiano nights or tech meetups. Ingest Tier A sources, plus Instagram Business Discovery on a curated list of handles, plus an organiser self-submission form. Ship it as a **weekly WhatsApp Channel and email digest of 5–10 picks**, backed by a lightweight web page that emits its own Event JSON-LD for Google. That format fits the weekend-peaking usage pattern, users' stated preference for a handful of picks over a firehose, and SA's data costs. One-to-many WhatsApp Channels cost nothing per message, unlike personalised Business API alerts, whose SA pricing the research did not cover. Add UTM tags to every outbound ticket link from day one: click-through data is the only leverage you will have when you ask Quicket (now Ticketmaster), Howler or Computicket for a referral fee or a data licence.

## Conclusion

SA has no discovery problem in the sense of missing information. Its problem is that the information sits behind different gatekeepers: supermarket tills, a Live Nation duopoly, independent festival platforms, and Instagram accounts that deliberately avoid mass marketing. An aggregator that scrapes the ticketing platforms would still miss the events a curious local wants most, because those events never reach a ticketing marketplace. Curators who know where to look add the most value here, and a scraper adds the least. Unusually, the personal and commercial versions of the project converge. The script you would build to fix your own feed (JSON-LD sources, followed Instagram handles, an LLM to read posters, a weekly digest) is the minimum viable product. Running it publicly for your city for a few months is the cheapest way to learn whether anyone besides you wants it.

Several questions remain open and should be tested directly rather than assumed. Does Quicket's API expose cross-organiser listings? Will Ticketmaster extend an affiliate route to Quicket inventory? What do the platforms' ToS actually say about scraping? And does a WhatsApp Channel for events retain an audience at SA scale? None of these is answered in published sources. Each costs an email or a month of running the digest to find out.
